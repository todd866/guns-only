## 2. The industrial thesis

**Proposed production baseline:** Rapier is not made by one secret workshop. A state-owned common
authority owns the product definition, while a multinational supplier network feeds two mirrored
final-assembly lines, two hot-structure sources, two propulsion integration sources, and a
government-controlled depot and rotable pool.

The expensive object is the **production-and-sustainment network**. The aircraft is the network's
repeatable, repairable output. The line, depots, data, and rotable pool provide strategic depth;
the aircraft remains a recoverable asset rather than planned ammunition.

The baseline has six rules:

1. The common authority owns the complete technical-data package, software release chain, process
   qualification baseline, special tooling definition, repair data, and configuration ledger.
2. No national variant changes structure, propulsion, flight controls, capsule, launch fitting, or
   maintenance interfaces. National mission software and crypto remain replaceable modules.
3. A second source is real only if it builds accepted hardware every year. A dormant licence or a
   paper-qualified vendor is not resilience.
4. No single plant supplies more than half of complete airframes, complete propulsion modules, or
   complete CMC hot sets at mature rate.
5. Production, depot activation, spares, and technical data are funded before the fleet reaches
   operational scale.
6. Ordinary cold composite work is dispersed. Special CMC furnaces, engine test cells, and
   airworthiness skills are duplicated and stocked because they cannot honestly be made mobile.

The external policy analogue is the US Department of Defense
[National Defense Industrial Strategy](https://www.businessdefense.gov/docs/ndis/2023-NDIS.pdf),
which calls for multiple, redundant production lines and allied co-production and co-sustainment.
That strategy supports the network pattern, not any Rapier programme number.

## 6. Correct CMC manufacturing story

### 6.1 What the line does

**Proposed production baseline:** flight-critical SiC/SiC panels, edges, lips, liners, duct sections,
and nozzle parts are **not simply printed**. The credible 2040 route is:

1. qualify SiC fibre, weave/tape, interface chemistry, matrix feedstock, coating feedstock, and
   witness-coupon plan by lot;
2. weave, braid, tape-lay or otherwise form a fibre preform on controlled tooling;
3. apply the fibre/matrix interface, such as a boron-nitride-class interphase, using the selected
   qualified process;
4. densify by chemical-vapour infiltration, polymer infiltration and pyrolysis, melt infiltration,
   or a qualified hybrid;
5. repeat infiltration/pyrolysis cycles where required to reach the controlled density and porosity;
6. diamond-grind, laser-machine, drill, join and finish only with qualified procedures;
7. apply the environmental/thermal coating system where the component requires it;
8. inspect by the approved combination of radiography or CT, ultrasound, thermography, dimensional
   metrology, surface inspection, and companion witness-coupon testing;
9. preserve the fibre, interface, furnace, cycle, coating, machining and NDI pedigree against the
   component serial number.

NASA's primary manufacturing survey describes CMC preforming followed by CVI, PIP, melt
infiltration, or hybrid processes, plus machining, coating, joining and NDE; it also notes that CVI
of large objects can take weeks to months
([NASA, *Additive Manufacturing of SiC-Based CMCs*](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160010285/downloads/20160010285.pdf)).
NASA's durability review illustrates SiC fibre, woven preforms, interface deposition, matrix
deposition, slurry and silicon infiltration routes
([NASA/TM-2018-219884](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20180002984/downloads/20180002984.pdf)).

### 6.2 Where additive manufacturing belongs

Additive manufacturing may produce:

- tooling, mandrels, furnace fixtures and sacrificial forms;
- qualified monolithic SiC vanes, shields, local inserts, or repair blanks;
- metal fuel nozzles, manifolds, brackets, ducts and low-count complex hardware;
- process-development coupons and inspection artefacts.

Fibre-reinforced CMC additive manufacture remains a qualification burden, not a magic replacement
for fibre architecture and densification. NIST notes that safety-critical additive parts require
formal qualification of machine capability, feedstock, post-processing, material properties,
microstructure, dimensional metrology and NDE
([NIST Additive Manufacturing Part Qualification](https://www.nist.gov/programs-projects/additive-manufacturing-part-qualification)).

### 6.3 Why two hot-structure sites are plausible

GE's official CMC industrial examples include a **$200M** two-factory campus for large-scale SiC
fibre and prepreg production
([GE Aerospace, Huntsville](https://www.geaerospace.com/news/press-releases/other-news-information/ge-aviation-takes-occupancy-one-kind-huntsville-factory))
and a **$125M**, 170,000-square-foot, approximately 340-worker factory created to mass-produce CMC
engine components
([GE Aerospace, Asheville](https://www.geaerospace.com/news/hu/press-releases/other-news-information/ge-aviation-opens-unique-composites-facility-asheville-nc)).
Those are maturity and facility-scale analogues only. Rapier's large airframe hot panels, joints,
cycle, yield and cost require their own qualification.

## 7. Buried capsule and escape-system supply

The occupant capsule is buried **inside the aircraft's forward centrebody**, not expressed as an
external spine and not underground. It is both the pressure enclosure and the proposed escape body.
Chapter 50 leaves the jettison sequence,
separation dynamics, recovery method, survival rate, and operating envelope open. This supply-chain
baseline must not close those claims by implication.

### 7.1 Controlled subsystem

`F-SCS` supplies one serialized, acceptance-tested capsule shipset containing:

- toughened composite pressure shell and bonded/isolated structural hardpoints;
- reclined occupant support, restraint, shock attenuation and anthropometric adjustment;
- pressure regulation, oxygen or breathing-gas interfaces, thermal control and emergency reserves;
- displays, controls, internal lighting, audio, communications and camera/data interfaces;
- essential battery and FBW/escape hold-up power;
- fire, pressure, atmosphere and structural-health sensing;
- separation fittings, safing/arming and initiation electronics to an authority-owned interface;
- recovery-pack, flotation, beacon and post-separation support **only after those architectures are
  selected and qualified**.

The capsule supplier may not substitute shell resin, reinforcement, hardpoint, initiator,
restraint, life-support valve, battery chemistry, release logic, or recovery hardware without
common-authority approval.

### 7.2 Manufacture and acceptance

**Proposed production baseline:**

1. manufacture the pressure shell and bonded hardpoints under a controlled composite process;
2. inspect cure, bondline, inserts, penetrations and impact history;
3. proof-pressure and leak-test every shell;
4. functionally test atmosphere control, emergency power, displays, restraint and communication;
5. execute hardware-in-the-loop tests of safing, release command, power-loss and false-command cases;
6. rig-check the physical jettison path before the capsule is buried under aircraft fairings;
7. perform lot-based destructive shell, restraint and initiator testing;
8. preserve an as-built life ledger through storage, installation, flight, removal and depot work.

The capsule is a rotable only while its shell, initiators, pressure equipment, batteries, restraint,
software and recovery components remain inside their individual lives. A capsule moved from an
expired airframe shell does not inherit a fresh life merely because its paint looks intact.

### 7.3 Industrial resilience

The pressure shell and integration cells are duplicated, but escape initiation and release
electronics remain a tightly controlled source family. The common authority holds:

- complete drawings, software, interface and acceptance data;
- golden test equipment and duplicate calibrated rigs;
- controlled initiator and battery stocks;
- a second qualified assembly cell that produces annual accepted hardware;
- an independent accident-investigation and destructive-test capability.

This makes capsule supply visible as a pacing airworthiness item rather than hiding it in the
generic avionics row.

The US Government Accountability Office identifies foreign dependence, weak lower-tier visibility,
castings and forgings, microelectronics, and aviation supply as persistent defence-industrial risks
([GAO-25-107283](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-107283/index.html)). Rapier therefore records
country of origin and actual producing facility below tier one rather than accepting a prime's
unexpanded supplier name.

### 8.2 Stocking policy

**Proposed production baseline:**

- 18 months of SiC fibre, interface and coating feedstock;
- 12 months of turbine forgings, critical bearings, trusted electronics, capsule initiators and
  long-life batteries;
- six months of ordinary composite, sealant, fastener, filter and fluid supply;
- one complete government-owned toolset and golden inspection standard beyond active production;
- offline signed technical releases and recoverable configuration records in three jurisdictions;
- warm second sources receiving recurring production, not only emergency qualification exercises.

## 9. Machinery and process needs

### 9.1 Cold-structure cells

- automated fibre-placement and/or overbraid systems;
- resin-infusion and OoA prepreg tooling, vacuum systems and large controlled ovens;
- laser projection, ply inspection and material freezer control;
- five-axis trim/drill, dust extraction and bonded-joint preparation;
- ultrasonic, thermographic and dimensional inspection;
- proof fixtures for carry-through, gear, hook, launch and actuator loads.

NASA operates automated fibre-placement research through its ISAAC robotic cell
([NASA Langley ISAAC](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240002141)), and AFRL has demonstrated
automated overbraid and alternative tooling for large composite inlet ducts
([AFRL inlet-duct manufacturing](https://afresearchlab.com/news/afrl-associates-improve-processes-for-fabricating-aircraft-engine-inlet-ducts/)).
These are process analogues, not Rapier qualification.

### 9.2 CMC cells

- fibre handling, weave/braid/tape preform and interface-coating equipment;
- CVI, PIP, MI or hybrid furnaces and their gas, vacuum, thermal and environmental controls;
- diamond grinding, laser machining, controlled drilling and ceramic-compatible joining;
- environmental/thermal coating preparation and application;
- CT/radiography, ultrasound, thermography, microscopy and coupon laboratories;
- furnace-cycle data capture and material-property allowables control.

### 9.3 Propulsion cells

- precision turning, milling, grinding and inspection;
- qualified cast/forge/HIP/coating supply;
- additive metal machines only for qualified part families;
- rotor balance and overspeed/spin capability;
- fuel, lubrication, starter/generator and controls benches;
- inlet/combustor/nozzle direct-connect rigs;
- full-engine sea-level and altitude-condition test capability;
- teardown, borescope, metrology and materials laboratory.

DARPA's completed AFRE programme describes full-scale inlet, combustor, turbine/common-nozzle and
integrated TBCC ground testing
([DARPA AFRE](https://www.darpa.mil/research/programs/advanced-full-range-engine)).
Hermeus reports a turbojet-to-ramjet transition in its Chimera demonstrator and use of an available
turbine core as a development strategy
([Hermeus Chimera](https://www.hermeus.com/chimera)). Those sources show the development pattern;
they do not establish Rapier's engine performance, cost, service life, or readiness.

### 9.4 Integration and acceptance cells

- metrology-controlled body and wing fixtures;
- propulsion insertion/removal and hot/cold isolation tooling;
- capsule handling and jettison-path rig;
- fuel, pressure, drainage and electrical test rigs;
- FBW and mission-system hardware-in-the-loop laboratories;
- EMI/EMC and environmental test;
- engine-run enclosure, taxi surface and conventional acceptance runway;
- transport cradles and rail/road loading equipment.

## 10. Transport and final assembly

### 10.1 Module movement

**Proposed production baseline:**

- forward, centre and aft body modules move in enclosed road/rail fixtures before backbone mate;
- wings, fins and leading-edge hot inserts move separately;
- turbine core, ram/bypass hot kit, nozzle, capsule and avionics travel as controlled rotables;
- CMC components use shock, humidity and handling-event monitors;
- engine and capsule containers preserve desiccation, cleanliness, tamper and configuration seals;
- rough forgings and raw feedstock move before wartime transport constraints become acute.

The complete 7.35 m-span aircraft is an outsize road load and does not fit ordinary rail loading
gauges. Final assembly therefore occurs beside an acceptance runway and rail or heavy-road spur.

### 10.2 Delivery decision

**Open decision:** the repo does not close a conventional runway takeoff roll or a field-removable
wing joint.

The preferred industrial option is:

1. conduct a low-mass conventional first flight from a long production acceptance runway;
2. ferry accepted aircraft to regional depots or operating lanes;
3. retain wing-off heavy transport as battle-damage and denied-airspace contingency;
4. qualify any removable wing joint and re-mate inspection before relying on that contingency.

If conventional first flight cannot be closed, each production region needs access to a qualified
launch lane or the programme must accept wing-off delivery and local final mate. Neither cost is
currently in the $9M flyaway.

### 10.3 Mate flow

The Plate 00 order remains:

1. join forward, centre and aft cold backbone;
2. install propulsion tunnel and seal controlled CMC joints;
3. install the accepted buried capsule inside the forward centrebody;
4. attach wings;
5. install fuel, avionics, bay, gear, hook, launch fitting and remaining systems;
6. attach the selected directional-control assembly after the tail trade closes;
7. inspect, test, weigh, run and fly.

The current sequence creates a maintainability risk because the duct is sealed before centrebody
access closes and before the wings are mated. The **proposed production baseline** divides
propulsion into:

- a fixed inlet/liner structural path;
- an upper-removable turbine-core cassette;
- an aft-removable ram-burner/nozzle hot kit.

That split must be proven against the closed OML, load paths and 1,100 kg provisional module-E mass.

## 11. Production cadence and workforce

### 11.1 Rate plan

| Period | Proposed production baseline | Purpose |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| 2026–2030 | Material, propulsion, capsule, inlet and process demonstrators | Close pacing technologies and production recipes |
| 2031–2034 | 6→24 aircraft/year | Engineering and manufacturing development; destructive and flight qualification |
| 2035–2037 | 36→72/year | Low-rate production and depot activation |
| 2038–2040 | 96→144/year | Two-line mature ramp |
| Mature steady state | **144 aircraft/year** | Six per month from each active final line |
| Mobilised surge | **up to 192/year assembly capacity** | Two-shift active lines plus the existing 48/year shadow-line activation after 12–18 months; upstream capacity remains a closure gate |

Supporting mature annual capacity:

- **170–180 complete propulsion modules**, including production installs and spare rotables;
- approximately **180 complete CMC hot sets**, including production, qualification and depot stock;
- capsule output above airframe rate so acceptance failures, depot exchange and destructive lot tests
  do not stop final assembly;
- rotables and repair capacity funded from the first production lots.

Those propulsion and hot-set capacities support the **144/year mature line plus a modest rotables
margin**; they do not support the stated 192/year assembly ceiling. Mobilising all three final lines
therefore requires at least approximately **210 propulsion modules and 210 hot sets per year**, with
capsule and specialist-tier capacity scaled above that. **240 aircraft/year is rejected as a
current baseline:** it would require another or expanded final line and upstream capacity above
approximately 260 complete shipsets/year, not merely extra shifts at the named sites.

Aircraft output is not directly convertible into sortie capacity. The required annual rate must be
solved from fleet size, availability, mission mix, measured component consumption, combat and
accident loss, inspection findings, repair yield, depot turnaround, and rotable policy. Chapter
[85 — Service life, maintenance, and telemetry](85-service-life-maintenance-and-telemetry.md)
defines the severity-weighted component record that this industrial model must eventually consume.

## 12. Quality, configuration, and production acceptance

### 12.1 Digital birth record

Every serialized airframe, propulsion module, hot component, capsule, gear set, hook, launch fitting,
actuator, computer and pressure vessel carries:

- material lot, country of origin and actual producing site;
- machine, tool, operator/automation cell and approved process revision;
- cure, furnace, coating, heat-treatment and machining history as applicable;
- witness-coupon identity and results;
- dimensional and NDI record;
- software, firmware, crypto and calibration baseline;
- concession, repair and deviation history;
- installed-airframe history and measured fatigue/thermal/cycle life.

Government ownership of this record is a deliberate response to real sustainment failures where
technical-data rights and parts accountability obstruct organic repair. The primary external
analogue is GAO's review of
[weapon-system sustainment data rights](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-107468/index.html).

### 12.2 Supplier and module acceptance

- incoming feedstock verification and source trace;
- first-article approval for each supplier, machine family, tool and major process change;
- statistical process control plus lot witness coupons;
- CMC density/porosity/property acceptance tied to validated NDI;
- composite cure, bond, impact and void inspection;
- forging/casting pedigree, heat treatment and NDI;
- capsule pressure, leak, restraint, life-support, power and release-path tests;
- engine balance, overspeed/spin, full-power and control acceptance;
- fuel/RCS cleanliness, pressure, flow and leak tests;
- HIL, EMC and environmental test for flight/mission electronics;
- proof loading for landing, hook and launch fittings.

NASA has used x-ray, ultrasonic and thermographic methods to interrogate SiC/SiC combustor
components before and after rig testing
([NASA CMC NDE](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/archive/nasa/casi.ntrs.nasa.gov/20090007545.pdf)).
The lifecycle programme analogue is
[MIL-HDBK-6870](https://quicksearch.dla.mil/qsDocDetails.aspx?ident_number=121258), which provides
US DoD guidance for aircraft and missile NDI programme plans.

### 12.3 Complete-aircraft acceptance

1. closed-OML and control-surface dimensional survey;
2. final mass and CG against the controlled build state;
3. pressure, fuel, RCS, drainage and fluid integrity;
4. flight-control BIT, HIL release, actuator sweep and failure injection;
5. capsule atmosphere, emergency power, displays, safing and jettison-path checks;
6. landing-gear swing, hook drop, bay door/retention and launch-fitting proof;
7. engine ground run and inlet/control health;
8. software, firmware, crypto, serial and life-ledger reconciliation;
9. taxi and braking acceptance;
10. limited low-life first flight and delivery inspection;
11. lot-sampled full-envelope/high-Mach qualification rather than consuming a hard-pull fraction on
    every production article.

No production acceptance card may claim sustained Mach 4, qualified whole-aircraft 1,200 °C
operation, a closed escape survival envelope, or qualified component lives and inspection
intervals until those programmes produce evidence.

## 13. Depot, rotables, and field support

### 13.1 Three maintenance levels

| Level | Permitted work | Explicit boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Operating lane | Service fuel, ammunition, gases and fluids; inspect; load software; replace panels, filters, wheels/brakes, sensors, actuators and approved line-replaceable modules | No turbine teardown, CMC furnace repair, unapproved capsule opening, or structural bond improvisation |
| Regional depot | Exchange propulsion, hot kit, capsule, avionics, wing, gear and hook modules; perform damage assessment, mobile NDI, approved composite repair, shell harvest and return-to-flight acceptance | No unqualified CMC densification/coating or rotor-life extension |
| Deep depot | Engine teardown and rotor/coating work; CMC furnace, coating and destructive investigation; major structural repair; capsule overhaul and accident investigation; tooling/process requalification | Not a sortie-turnaround facility |

No service task puts personnel in the launch bore while stored launch energy is available, consistent
with chapter 82.

### 13.2 Initial rotable seed

**Proposed production baseline per 24-aircraft operational package:**

| Rotable or stock | Initial planning seed |
| --- | ---: |
| Complete TBCC propulsion modules | 4 |
| Complete inlet/ram/nozzle hot kits | 4 |
| Capsule shipsets | 2 |
| Avionics/FBW/sensor rack sets | 6 |
| Gear/hook shipsets | 4 |
| Launch-fitting/holdback hardware sets | 4 |
| Approved actuator, pump, valve, wheel/brake and pressure-vessel pool | Reliability-model driven |
| Lane consumables | 7 operating days |
| Regional rotables and consumables | 30 operating days |
| Strategic long-lead material | 12–18 months by family |

These are provisioning seeds, not availability claims. Reliability growth, repair turnaround,
sortie tempo, transport time, combat damage, commonality and cannibalisation data must replace them.

### 13.3 Asset ownership

The common authority or participating state owns aircraft and spare assets. The prime may operate
warehouses under contract but does not own an opaque global pool.

GAO found that spare shortages, repair backlogs, incompatible deployment kits, weak accountability,
and immature movement networks materially reduced real F-35 availability
([GAO-19-321](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-19-321)). Its later sustainment review again identifies
underinvestment in spares and depot activation as a readiness cause
([GAO-26-108113](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-108113/index.html)).
Rapier's provisioning and depot funding therefore begin before operational fleet growth.

## 14. Lifecycle economics

### 14.1 What the $9M claim means

The existing approximately **$9M flyaway** remains a **surrogate mature recurring unit cost**. Under
this baseline it means:

- aircraft hardware and normal production labour at 100-plus aircraft per year;
- government-funded production tooling already in place;
- no allocation of research, qualification, plant capital, depot activation, initial spares,
  launch/recovery infrastructure, combat loss, or wartime transport;
- no claim that early development or low-rate aircraft cost $9M.

### 14.2 Programme-level planning ranges

| Cost element | Proposed 2026-USD planning range | Status |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Airframe, propulsion, capsule, systems and flight qualification | **$8–15B** | broad conceptual programme basis |
| Duplicate plants, tooling, engine/CMC test cells, digital infrastructure and acceptance facilities | **$4–8B** | broad conceptual industrialisation basis |
| Initial depot activation, training, technical data and rotable inventory | **$2–4B** | broad conceptual support basis |
| **Development + industrialisation + initial support** | **$14–27B** | before buying the operational fleet or launch lanes |
| Early production aircraft | **$15–25M each class** | learning-curve planning, not a bid |
| Mature recurring flyaway | **~$9M class** | existing repo surrogate, conditional on rate and exclusions |
| Complete basing lane | **$190–650M each** | existing chapter 82 conceptual range; separate from aircraft |

The facility ranges are informed only at order-of-magnitude level by the external CMC plants cited
in §6 and by the real fact that a purpose-built international F-35 final-assembly/check-out facility
was reported as a roughly $1B investment
([US Department of Defense](https://www.defense.gov/News/News-Stories/Article/Article/603803/f-35-european-maintenance-sites-announced/)).
Rapier is smaller and simpler in some respects but adds novel TBCC and large hot-structure
qualification. No direct unit-rate transfer is valid.

### 14.3 Component life and recovered value

**Accepted programme doctrine:** Rapier is a long-lived carrier assembled from independently
life-limited components. Chapter
[85 — Service life, maintenance, and telemetry](85-service-life-maintenance-and-telemetry.md) is
the controlling system design.

- the cold primary shell, wing/carry-through structure, and directional-control attachments own
  their measured mechanical spectrum, environment, damage, inspection, and repair histories;
- each removable inlet lip, leading edge, liner, panel, seal/attachment set, and aft hot shipset
  owns its local thermal, oxidation/coating, impact, and joint history;
- turbine core, ram hot kit, capsule, avionics, gun, gear, hook, launch fittings, actuators, pumps,
  and RCS pressure equipment own independent serial lives;
- the first Block-0 forensic teardown occurs at **50 severe-mission equivalents**; that gate is not
  retirement;
- **250** severe equivalents is the provisional minimum production-shell requirement, **500** the
  production structural objective, and **1,000** an extension target available only after evidence;
- all four labels remain inactive until chapter 85's `SME-v0` reference spectrum exists and may not
  yet drive production, dispatch, inspection due dates, or cost;
- an expired or uneconomic shell is stripped at a regional depot;
- serviceable rotables are inspected, repaired as authorised, and installed in a new shell;
- remaining structural and hot components become test articles, repair stock, material-recovery
  candidates, or controlled scrap; and
- no component receives a fresh life merely by moving, repair does not erase history, and unknown
  history is never interpreted as zero damage.

The former flat-per-sortie depreciation sketch is retired. Until qualified lives, mission spectra,
repair yields, inspection costs, and salvage values exist, lifecycle cost remains an uncertainty
band rather than a depot business case.

## 15. Wartime vulnerability and geographic dispersion

### 15.1 Pacing vulnerabilities

1. SiC fibre/prepreg, interface chemistry and coating feedstock.
2. CMC furnaces, process recipes, coating lines and qualified NDI.
3. Turbine forgings/castings, bearings, coatings and full-engine test cells.
4. Capsule pressure-shell, initiator, battery, life-support and release-system supply.
5. Trusted electronics, IMUs, power devices, approved magnets and signing infrastructure.
6. Skilled CMC, NDI, turbine, capsule, airworthiness and configuration personnel.
7. Final-assembly electrical power, rail/heavy-road access and acceptance runway.
8. Instrumented high-speed acceptance airspace and direct-connect propulsion rigs.
9. Cross-border permits, customs, data release and transport corridors.
10. Aircraft/launcher coupling hardware, proof fixtures and calibrated gauges.

### 15.2 Resilience measures

- hot-structure and propulsion sources on separate grids and in separate jurisdictions;
- two active final lines, neither carrying more than half of mature output;
- a shadow line that produces real annual warm articles;
- three recoverable copies of authority-owned technical data, tooling definitions and golden
  inspection standards;
- complete shipsets and repaired rotables stocked ahead of bare shells;
- ordinary composite work spread among interchangeable cells;
- strategic feedstock and rough-forging stock held before finish machining;
- mobile NDI, module-exchange and configuration teams;
- universal modification kits across one common block baseline;
- workforce cross-qualification and duplicate instructors;
- no critical production or depot process whose only operator, recipe, rig, key or gauge exists in
  one building.

Dispersion does not mean every process is hidden or mobile. Large furnaces, engine cells and
acceptance runways remain visible strategic assets. Their protection comes from duplication,
inventory, repair, deception, and replacement capacity rather than a false claim of invulnerability.

## 16. Worldbuilding and gameplay consequences

The industrial network should be visible in the world:

- a Rapier serial records which shell works, CMC furnace lot, engine line and capsule cell built it;
- CMC batches and approved depot patches may have subtle finish variation without becoming random
  cosmetic skins;
- an old engine, capsule or gun can accumulate history across several structural shells;
- a failed witness coupon can quarantine a production lot before it becomes an unexplained fleet
  debuff;
- a missing bearing, initiator, power module, resin freezer, calibrated gauge, software key or NDI
  technician can matter more than tonnes of generic “supplies”;
- escorting an engine cassette, recovering a damaged capsule, moving a golden tool, reopening a rail
  corridor, qualifying a substitute resin, or finding a mismatched software block can be complete
  logistics and engineering stories;
- factory workers, inspectors, depot crews, train crews, test pilots, suppliers, procurement staff
  and nearby registered communities all bear consequences when rate is treated as combat power;
- a damaged aircraft may be worth recovering for its capsule, propulsion and rotables even when its
  shell will never fly again.

The game should never reduce this network to a single factory health bar. Material, capacity,
quality, transport, workforce, configuration, inventory and repair are different systems with
different failure signatures.

## 17. Open decisions before a production release

- exact aircraft/shuttle cradle, keel fitting, holdback, release and failed-release response;
- whether a conventional light-weight production takeoff is possible;
- field-removable wing joint, re-mate tooling and transport envelope;
- inlet/liner, turbine core and aft hot-kit removal paths;
- selected CMC fibre architecture, densification route, coating, joint system, allowables and
  inspection acceptance criteria;
- propulsion core source, compressor/turbine architecture, production life and overhaul concept;
- capsule shell, release, separation, recovery, life-support and occupant-survival qualification;
- flight-control redundancy architecture and common electronics baseline;
- component-level fatigue, thermal, cycle, calendar and storage lives;
- production block strategy and configuration retrofit rules;
- mature fleet size, operating lanes, sortie tempo and therefore required annual replacement rate;
- repair turnaround, no-fault-found rate, spare ratios and distribution stock;
- first-flight, lot-sampled high-speed and launcher-compatibility acceptance programme;
- export, technical-data, software-signing and allied production governance;
- quantified development, industrialisation, recurring and lifecycle cost risk.
