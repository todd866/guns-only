# The Long Way Home: September 1951

Status: source-backed treatment, pre-script lock  
Mode: fixed-history reconstruction  
Target running time: 22 minutes

## One-sentence treatment

A young VF-51 pilot launches from USS *Essex* for an ordinary armed-reconnaissance sortie, flies
his Panther through an unseen cable, and has to keep the damaged aircraft controllable long enough
to reach friendly territory and eject.

## Point of view

The story stays with Ensign Neil Armstrong in 1951. It does not borrow importance from Apollo 11,
explain what kind of man history says he became, or give him knowledge he could not have had in the
cockpit. He is twenty-one, trained, inexperienced in combat and occupied by the next physical
problem.

Major John Carpenter is not a mentor invented for the game. He is the documented flight lead and
USAF exchange officer. His dramatic function follows directly from the operation: remain with the
damaged aircraft, inspect what Armstrong cannot see and help decide whether a landing is defensible.

The carrier and recovery personnel are not a chorus. They are working people seen through actions,
hand signals, machinery and a few source-bounded details.

## Dramatic proposition

The incident is compelling because its decisions are technical and immediate:

- a nearly invisible cable removes a large part of the right wing;
- enough control remains to make survival possible but not comfortable;
- slowing the aircraft may consume the remaining lateral authority;
- ejecting over hostile territory is unacceptable;
- the damaged aircraft therefore has to be flown south before it can be abandoned.

The player does not decide whether the cable strike happened. The player discovers why the
documented response required skill.

## Movement

### I. Routine becomes narrow

The first minutes are crowded but not dramatic. Deck crew work close to a compact dark-blue jet.
The straight deck is wet or matte according to the eventual weather lock; machinery, exhaust and
hand signals replace exposition. The catapult shot is abrupt. At altitude the flight joins, crosses
the coast and descends.

Korea is beautiful at human scale: broad ridges, worked land, low settlements, large weather and
small machines. Nothing in the landscape announces that it is a trap. Carpenter leads the flight
into the target corridor. Formation keeping, terrain clearance, target identification and the
attack task progressively consume the player's attention.

### II. The machine becomes the story

The flight exits along the required valley line. The cable exists in the world before contact. It
is thin, physically placed and visible under honest rendering, but it competes with terrain,
airspeed and the player's task. The Panther intersects it.

There is no slow motion. Contact is a violent sound, a lateral impulse, departing structure and an
immediate roll. Control remains with the player. The first dramatic sentence is the aircraft
itself: asymmetric lift, increasing stick demand, airframe noise and terrain rising in the
windscreen.

Once the aircraft is temporarily stable, Carpenter moves across to inspect. The scene is allowed to
take time because formation flight in the damaged aircraft is itself the performance. Carpenter
reports only visible geometry. Armstrong can report only the control he is carrying and the
aircraft's response. Their conclusion is procedural: do not slow it for a landing.

### III. South

The middle-distance flight south is not filler. The damaged Panther continuously spends the
margin the player preserved after impact. Radio becomes sparse. Landmarks and the friendly boundary
replace a map-arrow chase. The player is not being asked whether to eject; the player is being
asked to deliver the aircraft to a place where ejection can reproduce the documented outcome.

Near Pohang/K-3, Armstrong aligns the aircraft away from people and valuable structures, enters the
disclosed greybox ejection bounds and completes the exposed preparation. The exact seat, envelope
and checklist remain blocked on the applicable Panther handbook and equipment record. The act has
no triumphant countdown. Armstrong reports that he is getting out. Carpenter acknowledges.

The seat fires. Aircraft, seat and pilot become separate physical bodies. Spatial continuity is
never broken by a decorative camera cut. Engine and airflow vanish into blast, then wind, risers
and canopy. The damaged Panther continues without him.

A later H-Gram synthesis says wind carried Armstrong back over land. Armstrong's interview says a
jeep from K-3 arrived as he landed, driven by his former flight-school roommate Goodell Warren. The
game may show an authored wind and parachute path only as reconstruction after source and rights
review; it does not attribute the drift detail to Armstrong or invent dialogue for either man.

The ending is ordinary. A jeep, a parachute collapsing onto worked ground, two young servicemen and
the sound of the aircraft now absent. After a short quiet interval, the debrief distinguishes:

1. what Armstrong later described and official records preserve;
2. what the game reconstructed because the record is incomplete;
3. what the player actually did in this run.

## Player arc

The player's emotional arc is produced by changing control relationships:

| Phase | Relationship to the aircraft | Primary feeling |
|---|---|---|
| Deck and launch | Follow a practiced system | Anticipation |
| Join and ingress | Place the aircraft precisely | Concentration |
| Attack | Spend attention on the task | Compression |
| Cable contact | React before understanding | Shock |
| Damaged stabilization | Learn a new aircraft in seconds | Technical fear |
| Inspection | Hold still enough to be helped | Exposure |
| Southbound | Preserve a shrinking margin | Endurance |
| Ejection | Deliberately leave the machine | Commitment |
| Descent and recovery | Become a person in the landscape | Relief without triumph |

## Fixed-history contract

All twelve beats occur in order. A route abandonment, missed cable contact, pre-boundary ejection,
fatal departure or failed ejection does not become alternate history. The reconstruction pauses and
restores the nearest checkpoint.

The restart language is clinical:

> The recorded sequence cannot continue from this state. Restoring the last reconstruction
> checkpoint.

The run still records player performance. Competence changes control margin, workload, damage
recovery, formation quality, checklist execution and debrief evidence—not whether history occurs.

## Visual and tonal contract

“Ghibli” is shorthand for craft and feeling, not an instruction to copy a studio or living
filmmaker. Production prompts use an original language:

- hand-painted gouache backgrounds and soft cel-shaped forms;
- mechanically exact aircraft, deck equipment and cockpit interactions;
- large expressive weather and wind visible in vegetation, sea and fabric;
- warm light used sparingly inside an otherwise cool Korean atmosphere;
- affection for imperfect machinery without romanticizing its military purpose;
- inhabited land shown as a place, not target texture;
- violence brief, physical and consequential rather than spectacular;
- quiet allowed to exist after maximum noise.

The landscape is never evidence of innocence or guilt. It is the physical world through which the
sortie passes.

## Source boundary

The event spine is supported primarily by Armstrong's NASA oral history, NHHC archival material and
the USS *Essex* record set identified in `source-register.json`. Exact date, aircraft subtype,
sortie loadout, detailed target run, cable installation, radio words and ejection procedure remain
locked or visibly disputed until the governed dossier closes them.
